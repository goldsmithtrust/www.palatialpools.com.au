Signs Your Pool Is Leaking

Pool owners often suspect a leak when they notice their water level dropping, but evaporation — especially in Melbourne's hot, dry summers — can cause significant water loss too. Here are the key signs that suggest an actual leak rather than normal evaporation:

1. Water Level Drops More Than Expected

Normal evaporation in Melbourne causes 3–7 mm of water loss per day in summer and 1–2 mm per day in winter. If your pool is consistently losing more than 5 cm (50 mm) per week, or you need to add water more than once per week, a leak is likely. Wind, low humidity, and full sun exposure can increase evaporation, so consider your pool's specific conditions.

2. Autofill Running Constantly

If you have an automatic water top-up valve (autofill) and you notice it running frequently — or your water bill has increased unexpectedly — the autofill may be masking a leak by continuously replacing lost water. Check your water meter with all taps off and the pool equipment shut down; if it's still ticking over, the autofill is running.

3. Air in the Pump Basket

Persistent air bubbles in the pump strainer basket or visible air being pushed from the return jets can indicate a suction-side plumbing leak. The leak draws air in when the pump is running, rather than losing water (which happens when the pump is off).

4. Wet or Soggy Areas Around the Pool

Unexplained damp patches, soggy ground, or unusually lush grass growth near the pool or along plumbing runs can indicate a subsurface leak. In Melbourne's clay-heavy soils, leaking water can take time to surface, so the wet area may appear some distance from the actual leak location.

5. Cracks in the Pool Shell or Deck

Visible cracks in a concrete or fibreglass pool shell are potential leak points. Even hairline cracks can allow significant water loss over time. Similarly, cracks or settlement in the pool deck (coping or surrounding paving) may indicate ground movement caused by water erosion from a leak beneath the pool.

6. Increasing Chemical Consumption

A leaking pool loses not just water but dissolved chemicals. If you're finding that chlorine and salt need constant topping up beyond normal consumption, the pool may be leaking and taking treated water with it.

7. Algae Growth Despite Adequate Chlorine

A leak that draws in groundwater can introduce contaminants, phosphates, and nutrients that promote algae growth. If you're fighting persistent algae despite maintaining correct chlorine levels, a leak may be the underlying cause.

The Bucket Test: DIY Leak Confirmation

The bucket test is a simple, free way to determine whether your pool is leaking or just evaporating. Here is how to perform it accurately:

  1. Fill a bucket (any plastic bucket will do) with pool water to about 2/3 full.
  2. Place the bucket on a pool step so it is partially submerged. The water level inside the bucket should be at approximately the same height as the pool water outside.
  3. Mark both water levels — use tape or a marker to indicate the water line inside the bucket and on the outside of the bucket (at the pool water level).
  4. Turn off the autofill if you have one. Leave the pump running normally.
  5. Wait 24 hours. Do not use the pool during this period.
  6. Compare the two levels. If the pool water (outside mark) has dropped more than the bucket water (inside mark), you have a leak. If both levels dropped the same amount, the loss is due to evaporation.
💡 Advanced bucket test: For a more diagnostic result, repeat the test twice — once with the pump running and once with the pump off (for 24 hours each). If the pool loses more water with the pump running, the leak is likely in the plumbing (pressure side) or equipment. If it loses more with the pump off, the leak is likely in the pool shell or fittings.

Types of Pool Leaks

Pool leaks fall into four main categories, each requiring different detection methods and repair approaches:

1. Structural Leaks

Cracks or breaches in the pool shell itself — whether concrete, fibreglass, or vinyl-lined. Concrete pools can develop cracks from ground movement, thermal expansion, or structural settling. Fibreglass pools can develop osmotic blisters or stress cracks. Vinyl liners can tear, puncture, or deteriorate at seams.

2. Plumbing Leaks

The most common type of pool leak in Melbourne. Underground pipes, joints, and fittings can fail due to ground movement (Melbourne's reactive clay soils are notorious for this), tree root intrusion, age-related deterioration, or poor original installation. Plumbing leaks can occur in suction lines (from skimmer/main drain to pump) or return lines (from filter back to pool).

Professional pool plumbing — joints and connections are common leak points

3. Equipment Leaks

Leaks from the pump, filter, heater, chlorinator, or other equipment. Common sources include pump shaft seals, filter multiport valve seals, heater unions, and chlorinator cell housings. Equipment leaks are usually the easiest to find and fix, as they are above ground and visible.

4. Fitting Leaks

Leaks around pool fittings — skimmer boxes, return jets, pool lights, hydrostatic relief valves, and main drain covers. These fittings penetrate the pool shell and rely on gaskets, sealant, or grout to prevent water from escaping around them. Over time, sealants degrade, gaskets compress, and fittings can work loose.

Professional Detection Methods

When you can't find the leak yourself (which is the case for most pool leaks, as they are underground or below the waterline), a professional leak detection technician uses specialised equipment to locate it precisely. Here are the main methods we use at Palatial Pools:

1. Acoustic Listening Equipment

Acoustic leak detection uses highly sensitive microphones and listening devices to detect the sound of water escaping from a pressurised pipe. The technician places listening sensors on the pool deck, around equipment, and along known plumbing runs. Each location is assessed for the characteristic hiss, gurgle, or rushing sound that a leak produces. By comparing the intensity at different points, the technician can triangulate the leak's location, often to within 30 cm.

This method is particularly effective for pressurised plumbing leaks and is non-invasive — no digging is required during the detection phase.

2. Pressure Testing

Pressure testing involves isolating individual plumbing lines and pressurising them with air or water using a specialised test rig. If a line holds pressure, it's intact. If it loses pressure, there is a leak in that line. By testing each line separately (skimmer line, main drain line, each return line, vacuum line), the technician can identify exactly which line is leaking.

Professional diagnostic equipment helps isolate leak sources

Pressure testing is the definitive test for plumbing leaks and is a standard part of any professional leak detection service.

3. Thermal Imaging

Thermal imaging cameras detect temperature differences on surfaces. Water leaking from a pool creates a cooler zone (in summer) or a warmer zone (in winter) compared to the surrounding dry ground. A thermal camera can reveal these temperature anomalies, showing the technician where water is saturating the soil beneath the pool deck or surrounding areas.

Thermal imaging reveals hidden water flow beneath pool decks and surrounding areas

This method is particularly useful for locating leaks under paving, decking, or concrete where water cannot be seen directly. It is non-invasive and can cover large areas quickly.

4. Dye Testing

Dye testing uses a syringe of concentrated, coloured dye that is slowly released near a suspected leak point. If there is a crack, gap, or opening in the pool shell or around a fitting, the dye will be visibly drawn towards and into the leak by the suction of escaping water. This method is precise enough to pinpoint the exact location of a leak to within millimetres.

Dye testing is typically used after other methods have narrowed down the general area of the leak. It is the final confirmation step, particularly for structural leaks and fitting leaks. The technician often uses a mask and snorkel (or underwater camera) to observe the dye's movement around submerged fittings and surfaces.

5. Electronic Leak Detection

For vinyl-lined pools, electronic leak detection equipment sends a low-voltage electrical signal through the water. An intact liner acts as an insulator, but at a tear or puncture point, the signal passes through the breach into the conductive water beneath. By moving a sensor across the pool surface, the technician can detect the exact location of even tiny holes or tears in the liner.

6. Endoscopic Camera Inspection

A flexible endoscopic camera can be inserted into plumbing lines to visually inspect them from the inside. This is particularly useful for identifying cracked pipes, root intrusion, collapsed sections, or joint failures without excavation. The camera provides real-time footage that shows the technician — and the pool owner — exactly what is happening inside the plumbing.

Endoscopic cameras allow non-invasive inspection of underground plumbing

What Happens After a Leak Is Found

Once the leak has been located and documented, the process typically follows these steps:

  1. Report and quotation: The technician provides a detailed written report identifying the leak location, type, severity, and recommended repair approach, along with a fixed-price quotation for the repair.
  2. Scheduling: Repairs are scheduled at a time convenient for the pool owner. Simple repairs (equipment, fittings) can often be done the same day or within 48 hours. Complex repairs (underground plumbing, structural) may require planning, council permits (if excavation affects property boundaries), and coordination with other trades.
  3. Repair execution: The repair is carried out by qualified technicians. For plumbing repairs, this may involve excavation, pipe replacement, and reinstatement of surfaces. For structural repairs, specialised materials and techniques are used.
  4. Verification testing: After repair, the technician verifies the fix by re-pressurising the repaired line or monitoring water levels over 24–48 hours to confirm the leak has been resolved.
  5. Reinstatement: Any disturbed surfaces (paving, decking, garden beds) are reinstated to their original condition.

Insurance Considerations

One of the most common questions we receive is whether home insurance covers pool leak repairs. The answer depends on the cause of the leak:

Typically Covered

  • Sudden and accidental damage (e.g., a burst pipe caused by a specific event)
  • Storm damage that cracks the pool shell or damages plumbing
  • Accidental damage during other approved works

Typically Not Covered

  • Gradual deterioration and wear (the most common cause of pool leaks)
  • Maintenance-related issues (old seals, corroded fittings)
  • Tree root damage (in most policies)
  • Ground movement and settlement (unless you have specific cover)
  • Consequential water loss (your water bill increase)
💡 Tip: If you suspect your leak may be covered by insurance, contact your insurer before authorising any repair work. They may require their own assessor to inspect the damage, and starting repairs before approval may void your claim. We can provide the detailed leak detection report and photos that insurers typically require to assess claims.
