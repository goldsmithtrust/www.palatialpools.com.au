What Is Salt Chlorination?

Salt chlorination is the most popular method of pool sanitisation in Australia — and for good reason. Rather than manually adding chlorine to your pool each week, a salt chlorinator generates chlorine automatically from ordinary dissolved salt (sodium chloride) in the water. The result is a consistent, reliable level of sanitation with less effort, lower ongoing costs, and water that feels noticeably softer on your skin and eyes.

In Melbourne, where we estimate over 70% of residential pools now use salt chlorination, the technology has become the default choice for new pool builds and a common upgrade for existing pools. Whether you have a fibreglass, concrete, or vinyl-lined pool, a salt chlorinator can be retrofit to virtually any system.

Despite the name "salt water pool," the salt concentration in a chlorinated pool is only about one-tenth that of seawater. At 3,000–5,000 ppm, you can barely taste the salt — it's comparable to the salt level in a human tear. The water is not corrosive at this concentration when properly balanced, and the chlorine produced is identical to what you would add manually from a bottle.

The Electrolysis Process Explained

At the heart of every salt chlorinator is a process called electrolysis — the same fundamental chemistry that has been used in industrial applications for over a century. Here is how it works in your pool, step by step:

Step 1: Salt Dissolves in Pool Water

Pool-grade salt (sodium chloride, NaCl) is added directly to the pool water. It dissolves completely and distributes evenly throughout the body of water. Once dissolved, the salt molecules separate into sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻). These ions remain in solution indefinitely — the salt does not evaporate, break down, or get consumed in any meaningful way. You only need to top up salt when water is lost through splashout, backwashing, or heavy rain dilution.

Step 2: Water Passes Through the Salt Cell

Your pool pump circulates water through the filtration system. The salt cell (also called the electrode or electrolytic cell) is plumbed inline after the filter, typically as the last component before water returns to the pool. As water flows through the cell, it passes between a series of titanium plates that are coated with a precious metal catalyst — usually ruthenium or iridium oxide.

Step 3: Electrical Current Triggers Chlorine Production

The chlorinator's control unit sends a low-voltage direct current (DC) through the titanium plates. This electrical current forces the dissolved chloride ions (Cl⁻) to give up their electrons at the positively charged anode plate. Two chloride ions combine to form molecular chlorine gas (Cl₂), which immediately dissolves in the passing water to form hypochlorous acid (HOCl) — the same active sanitising agent found in liquid chlorine and chlorine tablets.

The chemical reaction at the anode:

2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
The chlorine gas instantly reacts with water: Cl₂ + H₂O → HOCl + HCl
HOCl (hypochlorous acid) is the powerful sanitiser that kills bacteria, algae, and pathogens.

Step 4: Chlorine Sanitises and Regenerates

As hypochlorous acid does its sanitising work — oxidising bacteria, algae, and organic contaminants — it eventually breaks down and releases the chloride ion back into the water. This means the salt is effectively recycled. The chloride ion is available to be converted into chlorine again the next time it passes through the cell. This is why a salt chlorinator is sometimes described as a "chlorine recycling system."

💡 Key takeaway: A salt chlorinator does not eliminate chlorine from your pool. It generates chlorine on-demand from dissolved salt, providing continuous, automated sanitisation. The water still contains free chlorine — typically maintained at 1–3 ppm, exactly the same as a manually chlorinated pool.

Salt Cell Components & Function

Understanding the components of your salt chlorination system helps with maintenance and troubleshooting. There are two main parts: the salt cell and the control unit (also called the power centre or chlorinator box).

The Salt Cell (Electrolytic Cell)

The salt cell is the component that sits inline with your pool plumbing, typically installed after the filter and heater. Inside the cell housing, you'll find a series of parallel titanium plates or blades. These plates are coated with a precious metal oxide — most commonly ruthenium dioxide (RuO₂) or iridium dioxide (IrO₂). This coating is what enables the electrolysis reaction and is the reason salt cells have a finite lifespan; the coating gradually erodes with use.

Quality cells feature:

  • Self-cleaning (reverse polarity): Most modern cells reverse the electrical polarity periodically (every 4–6 hours) to prevent calcium scale from building up on the plates. This is the "clicking" sound you may hear from your chlorinator.
  • Flow sensor: A built-in or external flow switch ensures the cell only operates when water is flowing through it. Running a cell without water flow will overheat and damage the plates.
  • Temperature sensor: Some cells include a thermistor that reduces output when water temperature drops below efficient operating range (typically 16°C).
  • Clear housing: Many cells have a transparent housing section that lets you visually inspect the plates for calcium build-up without disassembly.

The Control Unit (Power Centre)

The control unit is the "brain" of the system. It is typically mounted on the wall near your pool equipment pad. Its functions include:

  • Output control: Adjusts the percentage of chlorine production (0–100%). In Melbourne, a typical summer setting is 60–80%, dropping to 20–40% in winter.
  • Timer integration: Many units have built-in timers or integrate with your pool pump's timer to coordinate chlorine production with filtration hours.
  • Diagnostics: Displays salt level readings, cell condition, flow status, and error codes. Higher-end models offer Wi-Fi connectivity for smartphone monitoring.
  • Super chlorinate mode: A boost function that runs the cell at 100% for 24–72 hours to shock the pool after heavy use, algae presence, or contamination events.

Ideal Salt Levels (3,000–5,000 ppm)

Getting your salt level right is critical to both efficient chlorine production and the longevity of your cell. Every chlorinator model has a specific operating range, but here are the general guidelines:

Salt Level (ppm) Status Action Required
Below 2,500Too Low — Cell may shut downAdd salt immediately. Cell can overheat and wear prematurely at low levels.
2,500–2,999Low — Reduced efficiencyAdd salt to bring within range. Chlorine production will be below optimal.
3,000–3,500Ideal for most chlorinatorsNo action needed. Most efficient operating range.
3,500–5,000Acceptable — Check your modelSome models prefer this higher range. Verify with your manual.
5,000–6,000High — Approaching limitDo not add more salt. May notice slightly salty taste.
Above 6,000Too High — Risk of corrosionPartially drain and refill with fresh water to dilute. Excess salt can damage equipment.

How to Calculate the Salt Needed

To raise the salt level in your pool, you need to know your pool's volume and the current salt reading. As a rule of thumb:

Formula: To raise salt by 1,000 ppm, add approximately 4 kg of salt per 1,000 litres of water.

Example: A 50,000-litre pool reading 2,500 ppm that needs to reach 3,500 ppm (an increase of 1,000 ppm) requires: 50 × 4 = 200 kg of pool salt.

Always add salt gradually, broadcasting it around the pool while the pump is running. Allow 24 hours for full dissolution and even distribution before retesting. In Melbourne's cooler months, salt may take longer to dissolve — running the pump for an extended period after adding salt helps.

Melbourne-Specific Considerations

Melbourne's water supply is notably soft (low in dissolved minerals), sourced primarily from protected mountain catchments. This is generally excellent for salt chlorination because there is less calcium to cause scaling. However, Melbourne water can also have lower total alkalinity, which means pH can be more volatile. Salt chlorination inherently raises pH over time, so Melbourne pool owners should keep a close eye on pH and have hydrochloric acid on hand for corrections.

Maintenance Schedule for Salt Cells

Proper maintenance extends the life of your salt cell and ensures consistent chlorine production. Here is the recommended schedule we follow at Palatial Pools for our regular maintenance clients:

Weekly

  • Test free chlorine and pH levels — adjust chlorinator output percentage if chlorine is outside 1–3 ppm
  • Check the salt reading on the control unit display
  • Ensure the pump is running for adequate hours (minimum 6–8 hours in summer, 4–6 in winter)

Monthly

  • Test salt level with an independent salt test strip or digital tester (don't rely solely on the chlorinator's reading, as it can drift)
  • Visually inspect the cell through the clear housing for calcium scale build-up
  • Test stabiliser (cyanuric acid) levels — maintain 30–50 ppm to prevent UV degradation of chlorine
  • Check total alkalinity (80–120 ppm) and calcium hardness (200–400 ppm)

Quarterly (Every 3 Months)

  • Remove the cell and inspect the plates. If calcium scale is visible (white, chalky deposits on the plates), clean the cell.
  • To clean: soak the cell in a solution of 1 part hydrochloric acid to 10 parts water for 5–10 minutes. The scale will fizz and dissolve. Rinse thoroughly with a garden hose before reinstalling.
  • Check the cell terminals and wiring connections for corrosion
⚠️ Important: Never scrape, chip, or mechanically remove scale from salt cell plates. This will damage the precious metal coating and dramatically shorten the cell's lifespan. Always use an acid soak for cleaning.

Annually

  • Professional inspection of the entire chlorination system (cell, control unit, wiring, flow sensor)
  • Calibrate the salt reading on the control unit against a laboratory-grade test
  • Inspect sacrificial anodes in the pool (if present) and replace if more than 50% consumed

Common Problems & Troubleshooting

Even well-maintained salt chlorinators occasionally develop issues. Here are the most common problems we encounter on Melbourne service calls and how to address them:

1. Low or No Chlorine Production

Symptoms: Chlorine reads 0 or very low despite the chlorinator running. Pool may start turning green.

  • Low salt level: The most common cause. Test salt independently and top up if needed.
  • Dirty cell: Calcium scale insulates the plates and blocks electrolysis. Remove and inspect; acid wash if necessary.
  • Low water temperature: Below 16°C, most cells produce minimal chlorine. Supplement with liquid chlorine during Melbourne winters.
  • Worn cell: If the cell is 5+ years old and still produces poorly after cleaning, the coating may be depleted. Time for a replacement.
  • Insufficient run time: The pump must run long enough for the cell to produce adequate chlorine. Increase run time before assuming a cell fault.

2. High pH (Consistently Above 7.6)

Symptoms: Cloudy water, reduced chlorine effectiveness, scale formation.

Salt chlorination inherently produces sodium hydroxide (a base) as a by-product of electrolysis, which raises pH over time. In Melbourne's low-alkalinity water, this effect can be pronounced. Regular addition of hydrochloric acid (typically 200–500 ml per week) is normal and expected.

3. "Check Salt" or "Low Salt" Error

Symptoms: Error message on control unit despite salt being within range.

  • The salt sensor may need calibration — check your manual for the calibration procedure
  • Water temperature can affect salt readings — cold water may show lower than actual
  • Dirty or corroded sensor contacts — clean the cell terminals
  • If the reading is consistently inaccurate, the cell may need professional service

4. "No Flow" Error

Symptoms: Chlorinator displays a flow error and shuts down production.

  • Check that the pump is running and producing adequate flow
  • Inspect the flow switch — it may be stuck, dirty, or failed
  • Check for blockages in the plumbing or a dirty filter restricting flow
  • Ensure all valves are fully open

5. Excessive Calcium Scale Build-Up

Symptoms: Frequent "service cell" alerts, visible heavy scaling on plates.

While Melbourne water is soft, calcium hardness can creep up over time as water evaporates and is topped up (mains water adds small amounts of calcium each time). If calcium hardness exceeds 400 ppm, scale formation accelerates. Consider partially draining and refilling to bring calcium hardness back into the 200–300 ppm range. Also check that the reverse polarity function is working correctly.

When to Replace Your Salt Cell

Salt cells do not last forever. The precious metal coating on the titanium plates gradually erodes with each electrolysis cycle. Here are the signs that your cell is approaching the end of its life:

  • Age: Most cells last 3–7 years. If your cell is over 5 years old and showing symptoms, replacement is likely.
  • Declining chlorine output: Despite clean plates, correct salt levels, and adequate run time, the cell cannot maintain chlorine levels.
  • "Replace Cell" indicator: Many modern chlorinators track the cell's operating hours and display a replacement alert when the estimated coating life is exhausted.
  • Visible plate damage: When you remove the cell for inspection, the plates appear thin, pitted, or the coating has visibly flaked away. Healthy plates have a uniform dark coating; worn plates look silvery or patchy.
  • Increasing acid demand: A failing cell may still produce some chlorine but become less efficient, producing more by-products that raise pH disproportionately.
