Why Do Pools Turn Green?

A green pool is an algae problem, plain and simple. Algae are microscopic plant-like organisms that are present in every body of water — including your pool water — at all times. Under normal conditions, your pool's chlorine sanitiser keeps algae in check, killing cells faster than they can reproduce. But when chlorine drops to zero or near-zero, algae seize the opportunity and multiply exponentially.

In Melbourne's climate, a pool can go from crystal clear to visibly green in as little as 24–48 hours during summer, particularly after a period of hot weather, heavy rain (which dilutes chemicals and introduces nutrients), or equipment failure.

The most common causes of a green pool are:

  • Chlorinator failure or salt cell depletion: The most frequent cause we see. A failing salt cell or malfunctioning chlorinator stops producing chlorine without the owner realising.
  • Insufficient pump run time: The pump must run long enough for the chlorinator to produce adequate chlorine and for the filter to remove debris. In summer, 8–10 hours minimum is recommended.
  • Low stabiliser (cyanuric acid): Without adequate stabiliser (30–50 ppm), UV from sunlight destroys free chlorine within hours. Melbourne's strong summer UV is particularly aggressive.
  • Heavy rain: Melbourne's sudden downpours can dump thousands of litres of unchlorinated, nutrient-rich rainwater into a pool, diluting chemicals and introducing phosphates that feed algae.
  • Extended absence: Holiday periods are a prime time for green pool problems, particularly if the pool is left unattended for 2+ weeks in warm weather.
  • Poor filtration: Old, channelled, or compacted filter media cannot trap dead algae and organic debris effectively, allowing the pool to remain cloudy even when chlorine is present.

Severity Levels: Assessing Your Pool

Not all green pools are equal. The severity of algae growth determines the treatment approach, the amount of chemicals needed, and the time to recovery. Here is how we classify green pool severity at Palatial Pools:

Level 1: Light Green (Teal / Cloudy Green)

Can you see the pool floor? Partially — the bottom is hazy but visible in the shallow end.

What's happening: Early-stage algae bloom. Chlorine has recently dropped to zero and algae is beginning to multiply. The water may have a slight green tint and feel slightly slimy on pool surfaces.

Recovery time: 24–48 hours with correct treatment.

DIY difficulty: Easy — most pool owners can handle this level.

Level 2: Medium Green (Opaque Green)

Can you see the pool floor? No — the water is fully opaque in the deep end, and only 30–50 cm visibility in the shallow end.

What's happening: Established algae bloom, typically 3–7 days without chlorine. Algae is growing on walls and floor. Filter pressure may be elevated from dead algae clogging the media.

Recovery time: 3–5 days with aggressive treatment.

DIY difficulty: Moderate — requires confidence with chemical dosing and multiple interventions.

Level 3: Dark Green (Swamp Green)

Can you see the pool floor? Absolutely not — the water is deeply opaque and dark green. The walls feel very slimy.

What's happening: Severe algae bloom, typically 1–3 weeks without sanitisation. Thick algae on all surfaces. Organic debris accumulating on the floor. Filter media likely saturated with dead organic matter.

Recovery time: 5–10 days with professional-grade treatment, potentially requiring a filter media change.

DIY difficulty: Difficult — large chemical volumes needed and multiple days of intensive management.

Level 4: Black / Dark Brown

Can you see the pool floor? The water is nearly black — you cannot see more than 5 cm below the surface.

What's happening: Extreme algae and bacterial growth, often with additional organic decomposition (leaves, insects, animal matter). Multiple weeks to months without treatment. The water may have a foul odour. Mosquitoes are likely breeding.

Recovery time: 7–14+ days. May require partial drain, acid wash, and filter media change.

DIY difficulty: Not recommended — call a professional. The chemical volumes are large and potentially hazardous.

Step-by-Step Recovery Process

Here is the professional recovery process that our technicians follow for a typical Level 2–3 green pool in Melbourne. This process applies whether you are treating the pool yourself or have called us in to handle it.

Step 1: Remove Debris

Before adding any chemicals, manually remove as much visible debris as possible — leaves, branches, insects, and any other solid matter. Use a leaf shovel or heavy-duty skimmer net. The more organic matter you remove before treatment, the less chlorine is wasted oxidising debris instead of killing algae.

Step 2: Test and Adjust pH

Test the current pH level. For chlorine to be effective, pH must be between 7.0 and 7.4 — ideally at the lower end (7.0–7.2) for shock treatment. Chlorine is approximately 3× more effective at pH 7.0 than at pH 7.8. Add hydrochloric acid if pH is above 7.4. Allow 30 minutes of pump circulation, then retest.

⚠️ Critical: Do not skip pH adjustment. If you shock a pool at pH 8.0, you will waste most of your chlorine — only about 20% of it will be in the active (hypochlorous acid) form. At pH 7.0, approximately 80% is in the active form. Getting pH right first is the single most important step.

Step 3: Shock Dose with Liquid Chlorine

Add liquid chlorine (sodium hypochlorite, 12.5% available chlorine) to bring the free chlorine level to 20–30 ppm — this is known as a "super shock." For a 50,000-litre pool, this requires approximately 10–15 litres of liquid chlorine for a Level 2 pool. Distribute the chlorine evenly around the pool with the pump running.

Step 4: Brush All Surfaces

Thoroughly brush the pool walls, floor, steps, and waterline with a pool brush. This breaks the algae's grip on surfaces, exposes it to the chlorine in the water, and removes biofilm. Pay special attention to corners, behind ladders, and around fittings where algae accumulates.

Step 5: Run the Pump Continuously

Run the pump and filter 24 hours a day until the pool clears. This is essential for distributing chlorine, filtering dead algae, and maintaining circulation. Do not turn the pump off during recovery — even overnight. For variable speed pumps, run at medium-high speed (not the lowest energy-saving setting).

Step 6: Backwash the Filter Regularly

As dead algae is filtered out, the filter pressure will rise. Backwash when pressure increases by 50 kPa (7 psi) above the clean baseline, or at least twice daily during active recovery. For very green pools, you may need to backwash 3–4 times per day initially.

Step 7: Retest and Re-dose

After 12–24 hours, test the chlorine level. If it has dropped below 10 ppm, add more liquid chlorine to bring it back to 15–20 ppm. The chlorine demand from a green pool is enormous — the algae, bacteria, and organic matter consume chlorine rapidly. It is common to need multiple re-doses over 2–5 days. Keep testing and dosing until the chlorine level holds above 5 ppm without dropping rapidly.

Step 8: Clarify and Vacuum

Once the water begins to turn from green to cloudy white/grey (a sign the algae is dying), add a quality clarifier to help fine dead algae particles clump together for easier filtration. After the water clears significantly, vacuum the pool floor to waste (bypassing the filter) to remove settled dead algae and debris.

Step 9: Rebalance Chemistry

Once the pool is clear, rebalance all water chemistry parameters to normal levels: free chlorine 1–3 ppm, pH 7.2–7.6, total alkalinity 80–120 ppm, stabiliser 30–50 ppm. Add stabiliser if it is low — this is often the underlying cause of the green pool and must be corrected to prevent recurrence.

When to DIY vs Call a Professional

You Can DIY If:

  • The pool is Level 1 or Level 2 (light to medium green)
  • You have access to liquid chlorine, acid, and a test kit
  • Your pump and filter are working correctly
  • You can monitor and re-dose over several days
  • You are comfortable handling pool chemicals safely

Call a Professional If:

  • The pool is Level 3 or Level 4 (dark green to black)
  • Your pool equipment is not working (pump failure, chlorinator issue)
  • You have tried DIY treatment but the pool is not clearing after 5+ days
  • The filter media is old (5+ years) — it may need changing before the pool can clear
  • You are not confident handling large volumes of chemicals safely
  • You suspect an underlying equipment problem causing the issue
  • The pool is a health hazard (mosquitoes breeding, foul odour, near-black water)

Prevention Strategies

Preventing a green pool is far easier and cheaper than treating one. Here are the strategies we recommend for Melbourne pool owners:

  1. Maintain consistent chlorine levels: Test at least weekly (fortnightly in winter). Keep free chlorine at 1–3 ppm year-round.
  2. Monitor your chlorinator: Check that your salt chlorinator is actually producing chlorine. A common trap is assuming the chlorinator is working because the display shows it's on — the cell may be depleted. Test the actual free chlorine in the water.
  3. Maintain stabiliser levels: Keep cyanuric acid at 30–50 ppm. In Melbourne's high-UV summer, unstabilised chlorine can be completely destroyed within 2 hours of sunlight exposure.
  4. Adequate pump run time: Minimum 8 hours per day in summer, 4–6 hours in winter. Adjust based on temperature and usage.
  5. Regular filter maintenance: Backwash when pressure rises. Change filter media every 5–7 years (sand) or 7–10 years (glass).
  6. Pre-holiday preparation: Before leaving for holidays, boost chlorine to 5–6 ppm, ensure the pump timer is set correctly, top up salt, and have a neighbour or your pool service company check on the pool after 1 week.
  7. After heavy rain: Test and rebalance chemistry within 24 hours of significant rainfall. Rain dilutes chlorine, lowers pH, and introduces nutrients that feed algae.
