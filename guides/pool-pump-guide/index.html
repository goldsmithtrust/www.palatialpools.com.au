Types of Pool Pumps Explained

Pool pumps are the engine of your pool's circulation system. They pull water from the pool, push it through the filter (and heater, chlorinator, etc.), and return it clean to the pool. There are three main types of pool pump available in Australia, and the differences between them primarily come down to motor technology, energy efficiency, and noise.

Single Speed Pumps

Single speed pumps have a simple induction motor that runs at one fixed speed — typically 2,850 RPM (revolutions per minute) on Australian 50 Hz power. The motor is either on at full speed or off. There is no in-between.

  • Power draw: 750W–1,500W (depending on pump size)
  • Purchase cost: $400–$1,000
  • Installed cost: $800–$1,800
  • Annual running cost: $800–$1,500
  • Noise: 65–75 dB at 1 metre
  • Lifespan: 8–12 years

Single speed pumps are the cheapest to buy but the most expensive to run. They are being phased out in favour of variable speed models in many states and territories. If you currently have a single speed pump, replacing it with a variable speed model is one of the most impactful energy upgrades you can make to your home.

Dual Speed Pumps

Dual speed pumps offer two operating speeds: high (full speed, similar to a single speed pump) and low (roughly half speed). The low speed option reduces energy consumption by approximately 60% while still providing adequate circulation for daily filtration.

  • Power draw: 750W–1,500W (high) / 200W–450W (low)
  • Purchase cost: $600–$1,200
  • Installed cost: $1,000–$2,200
  • Annual running cost: $400–$800
  • Noise: 55–65 dB at 1 metre (low speed is significantly quieter)
  • Lifespan: 8–12 years

Dual speed pumps were popular as a middle-ground option before variable speed technology became affordable. They're less common in new installations now but remain a good option for pool owners who want energy savings without the higher upfront cost of a full variable speed pump.

Variable Speed Pumps (Inverter Pumps)

Variable speed pumps use a brushless DC permanent magnet motor (similar to an electric vehicle motor) that can run at any speed from approximately 600 RPM to 3,000+ RPM. A built-in inverter controller allows precise speed adjustment, and many models are programmable to run different speeds at different times of day.

  • Power draw: 100W–1,800W (depending on speed setting)
  • Purchase cost: $1,000–$2,500
  • Installed cost: $1,500–$3,500
  • Annual running cost: $200–$400
  • Noise: 45–55 dB at 1 metre (at typical operating speed)
  • Lifespan: 10–15+ years
💡 The Affinity Law: The key to understanding variable speed pump savings is the "Pump Affinity Law." When you halve the speed of a pump, you reduce energy consumption by approximately 87.5% (energy varies with the cube of speed). This is why running a variable speed pump at low speed for longer hours is dramatically cheaper than running a single speed pump at full speed for fewer hours — even though both achieve the same total water turnover.

How to Size a Pump for Your Pool

Choosing the right size pump is crucial. An oversized pump wastes energy, creates excessive noise, can damage plumbing through high water velocity, and provides no better filtration. An undersized pump won't circulate water fast enough to maintain water quality.

Step 1: Calculate Your Pool Volume

If you don't know your pool's volume, use these formulas:

  • Rectangular: Length × Width × Average Depth × 1,000 = Litres
  • Circular: Diameter × Diameter × Average Depth × 785 = Litres
  • Kidney/freeform: Measure at longest and widest points, multiply together, then multiply by Average Depth × 750 = Litres (approximate)

Most Melbourne residential pools range from 25,000 to 70,000 litres.

Step 2: Determine Required Flow Rate

Australian standards recommend turning over the entire pool volume at least once in an 8-hour period. The formula is:

Required flow rate (L/min) = Pool volume (L) ÷ (Turnover time in hours × 60)

Example: 50,000 L ÷ (8 hours × 60) = 104 L/min

Step 3: Calculate Total Dynamic Head (TDH)

Total Dynamic Head is the total resistance the pump must overcome to move water through the system. It includes friction from pipes, resistance from the filter, heater, chlorinator, and any elevation change between the pool and equipment. A typical residential pool has a TDH of 12–18 metres. Higher TDH requires a more powerful pump.

Step 4: Match the Pump Curve

Every pump has a performance curve showing flow rate at different TDH values. Choose a pump that delivers your required flow rate at your estimated TDH. For variable speed pumps, this is where they excel — they can be adjusted to deliver exactly the flow rate needed, even as conditions change (e.g., as the filter gets dirty and resistance increases).

EPA Victoria Residential Noise Guidelines

Under the Environment Protection Act 2017 and associated regulations, residential noise from equipment like pool pumps is assessed against the following thresholds at the nearest affected property boundary:

  • Daytime (7 AM–10 PM weekdays, 9 AM–10 PM weekends): Noise should not be unreasonably loud or annoying. In practice, councils often apply a guideline of 55 dB at the boundary.
  • Night-time (10 PM–7 AM weekdays, 10 PM–9 AM weekends): Stricter limits apply. Most councils expect noise to be below 45 dB at the boundary.
💡 Practical advice: If your pool pump needs to run overnight (common with variable speed pumps running longer hours at low speed), a variable speed pump on its lowest setting is typically the only type that will comply with night-time noise limits. Position the pump as far from neighbours' bedrooms as possible, and consider a pump cover or acoustic enclosure for additional noise reduction.

Installation Considerations

Proper installation is critical to pump performance, efficiency, and longevity. Here are the key factors to consider:

Location

  • The pump should be installed as close to pool level as possible and within 3 metres of the pool where feasible. Every metre of elevation above the pool waterline reduces suction performance.
  • Ensure adequate ventilation around the pump — motors generate heat and need airflow for cooling.
  • Protect the pump from direct exposure to rain and pooling water. A dedicated pump shed or cover is ideal.
  • Consider noise — position the pump away from bedrooms (yours and your neighbours').

Plumbing

  • Use the correct pipe diameter. Undersized pipes create excessive friction and reduce pump efficiency. For most residential pools, 40 mm (1.5") or 50 mm (2") pipe is standard.
  • Minimise the number of 90-degree elbows — each one adds significant friction. Use 45-degree bends or sweeping curves where possible.
  • Install unions on both sides of the pump for easy removal during servicing.
  • Ensure the suction line has an adequate slope back towards the pool to allow air to escape.

Electrical

  • All electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician (a legal requirement in Victoria).
  • The pump must be connected to a dedicated circuit protected by an RCD (residual current device / safety switch).
  • Variable speed pumps require specific electrical connections — follow the manufacturer's installation guide.
  • Ensure adequate weatherproofing for all electrical connections in outdoor installations.
