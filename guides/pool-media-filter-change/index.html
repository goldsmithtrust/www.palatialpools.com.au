Why Filter Media Needs Changing

Your pool filter is the heart of your pool's circulation system. Every litre of pool water passes through the filter media multiple times per day, trapping dirt, debris, oils, dead algae, and microscopic particles. Over time — typically every 5 to 7 years for sand — the media degrades and loses its ability to filter effectively.

Here is what happens to filter media as it ages:

  • Erosion and smoothing: Fresh filter sand has rough, angular edges that trap particles. Over years of water flow and backwashing, these edges become smooth and rounded, allowing fine particles to pass straight through.
  • Compaction: The media bed compresses under its own weight and water pressure, reducing the spaces between grains where filtration occurs.
  • Channelling: Water finds paths of least resistance through the compacted media, creating channels where it flows freely without being filtered. You can often see these grooves when you open the filter lid.
  • Biological contamination: Old media can harbour bacteria, biofilm, and organic matter deep within the bed where backwashing cannot reach and chlorine cannot penetrate effectively.
  • Chemical saturation: Media absorbs oils, minerals, and organic compounds over time. Once saturated, it can no longer capture these contaminants and may even release them back into the pool.

In Melbourne, where many pools are surrounded by established trees (gum trees are notorious for dropping leaves, bark, and oils into pools), filter media can degrade faster than in cleaner environments. Pools near building sites or unsealed roads may also experience accelerated media wear due to fine dust and sediment.

Signs Your Media Needs Changing

Not sure if your filter media is due for a change? Here are the telltale signs our technicians look for during pool inspections:

1. Persistently Cloudy Water

If your water chemistry is balanced — chlorine, pH, alkalinity, and stabiliser are all within range — but the water remains hazy or cloudy, the filter is likely the problem. Worn media cannot trap particles smaller than 40–50 microns, leaving your pool looking dull regardless of chemical treatment.

2. Channelling Visible in the Sand Bed

Open the filter lid and inspect the top of the media bed. Healthy media should have a flat, even surface. If you see grooves, holes, or channels, water is bypassing the filtration process entirely. This is one of the most definitive signs that a media change is overdue.

3. Sand or Media Returning to the Pool

If you notice sand, glass, or media particles collecting on the pool floor — especially near return jets — this usually indicates a broken lateral (the slotted pipe inside the filter that prevents media from passing through) or media that has degraded into particles too fine to be retained. Either way, the filter needs attention.

4. High Filter Pressure That Won't Reduce

A clean, healthy filter typically runs at 50–80 kPa (7–12 psi). If your filter pressure gauge reads 100+ kPa even immediately after backwashing, the media is likely compacted or calcified and cannot be restored by backwashing alone.

5. Excessive Backwashing Required

If you're backwashing more than once per week during normal conditions, the media may be so degraded that it's loading up with debris far too quickly, or channelling is preventing effective backwashing.

6. Algae That Keeps Returning

A filter that cannot trap dead algae cells after chemical treatment allows those cells to re-enter the pool, where they can seed new algae growth. If you're fighting recurring algae despite adequate chlorine, suspect the filter.

💡 Pro tip: If your filter media is over 5 years old and you're experiencing any two of the above symptoms, a media change is almost certainly the most effective fix. Chemicals, clarifiers, and flocculants are band-aids — they cannot compensate for worn-out media.

Types of Filter Media Compared

When it's time for a media change, you have three main options. Each has distinct advantages depending on your priorities — filtration quality, lifespan, environmental impact, or cost.

1. Pool Sand (Silica Sand)

Traditional pool filter sand has been the industry standard for decades. It's the most affordable option and works well for most residential pools.

  • Filtration size: 20–40 microns
  • Lifespan: 5–7 years
  • Cost: Lowest — approximately $12–$18 per 20 kg bag
  • Weight: Heaviest option — a typical filter requires 100–200 kg
  • Pros: Cheap, widely available, proven performance
  • Cons: Shortest lifespan, coarsest filtration, heaviest to handle, can channel

Pool sand is graded to a specific size (typically #20 silica, 0.45–0.55 mm). Never use play sand, builder's sand, or beach sand — they are the wrong grade and will either pass through the laterals or compact into an unusable mass.

2. Recycled Glass Media

Glass media has become increasingly popular in Australia over the past decade. Made from recycled glass that is crushed and tumbled to remove sharp edges, it offers significant performance advantages over sand.

  • Filtration size: 3–5 microns (up to 10× finer than sand)
  • Lifespan: 7–10+ years
  • Cost: Moderate — approximately $25–$40 per 15 kg bag
  • Weight: 20% lighter than sand for the same volume
  • Pros: Superior filtration, longer lifespan, resists channelling and biofilm, environmentally friendly (recycled material), requires less frequent backwashing
  • Cons: Higher upfront cost

The negatively charged surface of glass media actively attracts and holds fine particles, oils, and organic matter that would pass straight through sand. Popular brands in Australia include Viron EcoPure Glass Media and FilterGlass.

3. Zeolite (Natural Mineral Media)

Zeolite is a naturally occurring volcanic mineral with a unique honeycomb-like molecular structure. It offers excellent filtration and has the added benefit of absorbing ammonia and chloramines from pool water.

  • Filtration size: 3–5 microns
  • Lifespan: 5–7 years (can be partially regenerated with salt soaking)
  • Cost: Moderate to high — approximately $30–$45 per 15 kg bag
  • Weight: Significantly lighter than sand (use approximately 50% of the weight)
  • Pros: Absorbs ammonia and chloramines (reducing "chlorine smell"), fine filtration, lighter to handle
  • Cons: Can break down faster in high-flow environments, more expensive, less widely stocked

Step-by-Step: What a Technician Does During a Media Change

A professional filter media change is a skilled job that typically takes 1.5 to 3 hours depending on filter size and access. Here is exactly what our technicians do:

Step 1: Preparation & Safety

The pump is switched off and isolated at the power supply. All valves are closed. The filter area is prepared with a tarp to contain old media and protect surrounding surfaces. If the filter is near garden beds, plants are covered to protect them from the old media and any residual chemicals.

Step 2: Drain the Filter

The drain plug at the base of the filter tank is removed to drain as much water as possible. This reduces the weight of the media significantly — wet sand can weigh 50% more than dry sand.

Step 3: Remove the Multiport Valve or Lid

The multiport valve (on top-mount filters) or the filter lid (on side-mount filters) is carefully removed. On top-mount filters, the plumbing connections must be disconnected — our technicians use union fittings where possible to make future servicing easier.

Step 4: Remove the Old Media

This is the most physically demanding part of the job. Old media is scooped out by hand or with a purpose-built vacuum, taking care not to damage the internal laterals (the slotted pipes at the bottom of the tank that distribute water and prevent media from entering the return line). For a large filter, this can involve removing 150+ kg of wet sand — one of the reasons we recommend professional service.

Step 5: Inspect Internal Components

With the tank empty, the technician inspects:

  • Laterals: Each lateral pipe is checked for cracks, breaks, or missing slots. Damaged laterals are the most common cause of media returning to the pool.
  • Centre pipe (standpipe): Checked for cracks and correct positioning.
  • O-rings and seals: All seals are inspected and lubricated or replaced as needed.
  • Tank interior: Checked for cracks, corrosion, or damage.

Step 6: Fill with Fresh Media

The centre pipe is capped (to prevent media from entering it during filling) and the tank is partially filled with water to cushion the laterals. New media is added gradually, distributed evenly around the centre pipe. For glass and zeolite media, a base layer of coarser gravel may be added first to protect the laterals.

Step 7: Reassemble & Commission

The valve or lid is reinstalled, plumbing reconnected, and all seals checked. The filter is set to "rinse" mode and the pump is started to flush dust and fines from the new media. After a thorough rinse (typically 2–3 minutes), the filter is set to "filter" mode and normal operation resumes. Filter pressure is noted as the new baseline "clean pressure."

⚠️ Important: After a media change, the water may appear slightly cloudy for 24–48 hours as residual fines settle. This is normal and will clear naturally with continued filtration. Do not add clarifier or flocculant during this period.
